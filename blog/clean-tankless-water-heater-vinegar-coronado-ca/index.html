Descaling a Tankless Water Heater With Vinegar, or Calling for a Flush

Vinegar can dissolve the scale inside a tankless heater, but only if the unit has isolation valves and your manufacturer allows the method.

By the Coronado Express Plumbing team · Published October 24, 2026

Does vinegar actually work, and is it safe for your unit?

Yes, white vinegar dissolves the mineral scale that coats a tankless heater's heat exchanger, and many manufacturers publish a vinegar flush as the standard cleaning method. The catch is that it only works when you can circulate vinegar through the unit, which requires service valves on the hot and cold lines. Without them, a plumber has to add valves first.

Check your manual before you start. Some units call for a specific cleaner, a specific amount, or a particular run time, and using something different can damage seals. If the manual says the unit must be serviced by a licensed professional, take that literally.

Signs your tankless heater is scaling up

Scale builds slowly, so the early signs are easy to miss.

Hot water takes longer to arrive or runs lukewarm

A coated heat exchanger transfers less heat, so the unit struggles to reach the set temperature.

Error codes about flow or temperature

Many units flag scale buildup or limited flow through a code. Check the manual for what yours means.

Reduced flow at taps

Scale in the inlet filter screen or the exchanger restricts water movement, and you notice weaker pressure on hot taps only.

Noises from the unit

Popping, rumbling or kettle-like sounds can mean water is boiling in small pockets under a layer of scale.

Higher gas bills with no change in habits

The unit burns longer to deliver the same heat when the exchanger is coated.

Vinegar flush at home versus a professional flush

Doing it yourself with vinegar

  • Needs a small submersible pump, two hoses, a bucket and the amount of white vinegar your manual lists.
  • Needs isolation valves already installed on the water lines to the unit.
  • Takes patience, because the vinegar circulates for the time the manufacturer specifies and then the unit needs a thorough rinse.
  • Works best as routine maintenance on a unit that is lightly scaled.

Hiring a plumber to flush

  • Includes valve installation if the unit has none, and checks gas, venting and condensate at the same time.
  • Better for a unit with heavy scale, repeated codes or a long gap since the last flush.
  • Reduces the risk of a mistake with the pump, hoses or gas shutoff.
  • Fits a unit under a closet or tight space where reaching connections is awkward.

A typical vinegar flush, step by step

Your manufacturer's procedure takes priority over any general list, including this one.

1

Shut down the unit

Turn off the power at the unit and close the gas supply valve, as the manual directs. Allow the unit to cool.

2

Close water valves and connect the pump

Close the isolation valves, open the purge ports and connect hoses so the pump can push vinegar in one port and return it to the bucket from the other.

3

Circulate the vinegar

Run the pump for the time your manual states. Watch the bucket, since the liquid often turns cloudy as scale dissolves.

4

Rinse and clean the filter

Drain the vinegar, flush with clean water, then remove and clean the inlet filter screen under running water.

5

Restore and test

Close purge ports, reopen water and gas, restore power, run a hot tap and check for leaks at every connection.

Why a thin layer of scale costs so much

Scale is a poor conductor of heat. Even a thin film between the burner and the water forces the unit to fire harder and longer to hit the same temperature, and the hotter metal underneath wears faster. Small pockets of scale also narrow the waterways, which is what makes flow and temperature codes appear.

This is why a light flush on a schedule beats a rescue flush later. A coating that is a few months old dissolves quickly in vinegar, while a thick, old layer can resist a single pass and may need a plumber's repeated treatment.

Mistakes to avoid when descaling

  • ✓Skipping the manual and guessing at the vinegar amount or the run time.
  • ✓Leaving the gas or power on while hoses are connected and the water valves are closed.
  • ✓Using bleach or harsh cleaners that the manufacturer does not list.
  • ✓Forgetting to rinse, which leaves vinegar in the water lines and a sour taste at the tap.
  • ✓Overlooking the inlet filter screen, which is quick to clean and often a source of flow complaints.
  • ✓Reopening the water valves too fast, which can push air and debris through the unit.

What moves the cost of a professional flush

The price comes from time, access and what is found, not from the vinegar. The price is clear before work starts.

Isolation valves

If the unit was installed without service valves, adding them is separate work that makes every later flush easier.

Degree of scale

A unit that has not been flushed in a long time may need a longer run or a second pass.

Unit location

A heater in an attic, garage rafter or tight closet takes longer to reach and connect.

Additional findings

Clogged filters, a failing condensate drain or a damaged vent connector add repair time.

Unit type

Condensing models include extra parts that must be checked while the unit is open.

Maintenance flushes are not an insured loss, so plan for this as an ordinary upkeep expense.

Keeping scale in check between flushes

Some homeowners reduce scale at the source with a whole-house filter or conditioner on the cold supply, and our page on water softener and filtration explains how those work and their limits. A conditioner does not remove the need for a flush, but it can lengthen the time between them.

The most effective habit is a calendar reminder. Write the date of each flush on the heater with a marker, and check the inlet filter screen at the same time. If the unit needs more than a routine cleanup, our tankless water heater service page describes the diagnosis.

Serving the other side of the bay

Scale is a regional issue, not a Coronado one. Homes in San Diego receive the same treated water from the City, and Chula Vista sits under different water districts, so the best interval between flushes depends on the water you actually use. Ask your local water utility for its hardness number and compare it with your manual's advice.

Coronado local note

Coronado note: why scale is likely here

The 2023 annual report from California American Water on its Coronado system lists the treated water as 145 to 280 ppm total hardness as calcium carbonate. Expressed in grains per gallon, that is 8.5 to 16.4, which the USGS scale places at hard to very hard. For a tankless heater, that range means mineral buildup is a realistic expectation, so plan a flush on the schedule in your manual and check it sooner if hot water performance drops.

Permit rules matter too. The Building Division's guidance for Coronado, Handout 301 dated March 2025, says a permit is required to install a water heater, and it names tankless units too. Routine descaling is maintenance, but if a flush leads you to move the unit, change its gas line or alter its vent, those changes are installation work. Follow the manufacturer's instructions throughout, and phone the Building Division on (619) 522-7331 before changing anything beyond the flush.
