Tankless Water Heater Exhaust Pipe: What Every Homeowner Should Know

The exhaust pipe is the part of a tankless installation that cannot be improvised, and the manufacturer's manual decides almost every detail.

By the Coronado Express Plumbing team · Published October 24, 2026

What the exhaust pipe does and who decides how it is built

A gas tankless water heater burns fuel very hot and very fast, and the exhaust pipe carries the combustion gases safely outside. Most modern units are direct vent, meaning a sealed pipe brings outside air in and another pipe sends exhaust out. The pipe material, the maximum length, the number of elbows and the distance from windows and openings are set by the manufacturer's installation manual and by local code.

That makes the exhaust the least flexible part of the job. You cannot swap a pipe type because it is cheaper, and you cannot bend the run to suit a wall. If the manual says a certain material, a certain slope and a certain termination kit, that is what goes in.

Direct vent versus room air: how the two approaches differ

Both exist, but the sealed version is the common choice for new installs because it does not depend on air inside the house.

Direct vent (sealed combustion)

  • Pulls combustion air from outdoors through its own pipe, often a concentric or twin pipe arrangement.
  • Keeps house air out of the burner, which helps in tightly sealed homes.
  • Usually allows the unit to sit on an interior wall, garage wall or closet with a short run outside.
  • Requires the exact termination kit and pipe types listed by the manufacturer.

Room air (open combustion)

  • Draws air for combustion from the space around the heater.
  • Needs enough open air volume or dedicated openings, per the manual and code.
  • Is more sensitive to nearby exhaust fans, tight construction and dust.
  • Is less common in new tankless models, so check what your unit supports before planning.

Five details inspectors and manufacturers look at

Termination clearance

The exhaust outlet must stay a set distance from windows, doors, vents and gas meters. The manual and the local code give the numbers, so measure before you choose a wall.

Pipe slope and support

Horizontal runs must slope as directed, usually back toward the unit on condensing models, so liquid drains instead of pooling.

Sealed joints

Joints are cemented, clamped or gasketed exactly as listed. A loose joint can leak exhaust gas into the house.

Length and elbow count

Every elbow adds resistance. Exceed the allowed equivalent length and the unit can throw errors or run poorly.

Combustion air path

The intake must stay clear of leaves, snow, insulation and stored items, and be located as the manual directs.

How a vent installation unfolds from start to finish

Knowing the order helps you spot shortcuts.

1

Pick the wall and measure clearances

The installer checks the exact termination point against the manufacturer's clearance chart, then marks the penetration.

2

Size the gas line

A tankless unit draws a large gas flow all at once, so the supply pipe is checked against the unit's rating before anything is cut.

3

Run and support the vent

Pipe is assembled dry first, sloped as directed, supported at the listed intervals, then joined with the approved method.

4

Connect condensate and test

On condensing units, the drain and neutralizer are installed. Then the unit is fired and the vent joints and flame are checked.

5

Permit inspection

The finished work is inspected by the city before the job is closed.

Warning signs that an existing vent needs a look

A tankless heater that has been running for a while can develop vent trouble. These signs are worth a professional inspection, and none of them are a reason to keep using the unit and hope.

Error codes about airflow

Repeated vent or ignition fault codes can mean a blocked intake, a long run with too many elbows or a loose joint.

Soot or staining at the outlet

Dark marks around the termination suggest incomplete combustion or exhaust that is not clearing properly.

Water dripping from vent joints

On a condensing unit, drips at a joint mean the slope or seal is wrong and the liquid is not reaching the drain.

Rattling or whistling in the pipe

Noise can point to a loose support, a partial blockage or a termination kit that is not seated correctly.

A carbon monoxide alarm that has gone off

Treat any alarm seriously, ventilate, leave the home and call 911 before anyone investigates the cause.

Gas smell or carbon monoxide alarm: act before you diagnose

Any sign of a gas leak or exhaust spillage is an emergency, not a repair project.

  • !If you smell gas, leave the building, then from a safe place call 911 or SDG&E.
  • !Do not flip light switches, use phones inside, or do anything that can spark.
  • !If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air and call 911.

What changes the price of a tankless vent job

Vent work is a big share of the total on a tankless install, and it depends on the building more than the heater. You should see the price in plain terms before anyone starts work.

Run length and routing

A short run through an exterior wall is simple. A long run through a ceiling, attic or around obstacles takes more pipe, support and labor.

Vent material

Stainless systems and specialty termination kits cost differently than plastic systems, and the manufacturer picks which one is allowed.

Condensate handling

Condensing units need a drain path and sometimes a neutralizer, which depends on where a drain is nearby.

Gas line upgrade

If the existing line is too small for the unit, the pipe has to be resized, which adds materials and inspection steps.

Wall and roof penetrations

Cutting through stucco, masonry or a roof requires patching and weather sealing afterward.

Insurance generally does not pay for a planned upgrade, and a vent problem that causes damage will be handled by your carrier under its own terms, so ask them directly.

What a homeowner can check and what stays with a licensed plumber

You can look at the outside termination every few months to make sure nothing blocks it, such as a bird nest, a stack of boxes or a climbing plant. You can also test your carbon monoxide alarm and replace its batteries on schedule.

You should not cut, glue, extend or reroute the vent pipe yourself, and you should not change gas piping. These are licensed jobs because a mistake can release exhaust or gas into the home. The installation page for tankless water heaters explains what a service visit covers, and plumbing installation work covers the gas and water piping around the unit.

Questions to ask before you approve a tankless install

  • ✓Which vent material and termination kit does the manufacturer list for this exact model?
  • ✓Where will the exhaust terminate, and how far is that from windows, doors and the gas meter?
  • ✓Is the existing gas line large enough for this unit, and who is doing the sizing?
  • ✓How is condensate being drained, and will the drain line be protected from clogging?
  • ✓Who pulls the permit and schedules the inspection?
  • ✓Will the old vent be removed or capped properly?

Neighboring cities, same manufacturer rules

Manufacturer instructions do not change at the city line. If you also own or manage a property in Chula Vista or San Diego, the vent rules from the manual apply there too, though each city handles permits and inspections its own way.

Coronado local note

Coronado note: permit and gas line sizing

Handout 301, issued by the Coronado Building Division in March 2025, states that water heater installation requires a permit, and tankless units are named explicitly. That matters for the exhaust because the vent is inspected as part of the installation, so a vent changed without a permit can show up later when a buyer's inspector asks for the paperwork. Call the Building Division at (619) 522-7331 if you are unsure.

The same handout notes that existing gas lines may need to be altered, and that alteration requires gas line sizing calculations by a licensed plumber. A tankless heater pulls much more gas at once than a tank, so the line feeding it may be too small. Plan for that calculation before you buy a unit, and you can check any plumber's license at cslb.ca.gov, where plumbing is class C-36.
