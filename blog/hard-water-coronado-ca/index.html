Hard Water in Coronado, CA: What 8.5 to 16.4 Grains per Gallon Does to Your Home

Hard water is not a health problem, but it shortens the life of heaters and fixtures, and the right response depends on how much it bothers you.

By the Coronado Express Plumbing team · Published October 24, 2026

What the numbers mean in a kitchen and a water heater

Hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium in water, and grains per gallon is one way to measure it. At 8.5 to 16.4 grains per gallon, water leaves visible scale on fixtures, film on glass and mineral crust inside heating elements and tank bottoms. Over years, that can shorten appliance life and cut efficiency.

Hard water is mainly a nuisance and a maintenance cost, not a safety issue. Soap lathers less, shower doors cloud over and kettles furr up. Whether you treat it comes down to how much cleaning and appliance wear you are willing to tolerate, which is what the rest of this guide sorts out.

Signs the water at your tap is hard

Most households recognize these without testing, and several together are a clear pattern.

White crust on faucets and showerheads

Chalky buildup around aerators and spray holes narrows flow and sprays water sideways.

Spots on dishes and glass

Dried droplets leave cloudy rings that return after every wash, even with a rinse aid.

Stiff laundry and dull colors

Minerals can leave fabric feeling rough and make soap rinse out poorly.

Soap that will not lather

You use more shampoo or detergent for the same suds, and a scummy film forms in tubs and sinks.

Noisy or slow-heating water heater

Sediment collects at the base of a tank and can cause rumbling and slower recovery over time.

Where hardness hits hardest

Not every part of the house suffers equally. Heat accelerates scale, so hot water systems feel it first.

Softener, conditioner or doing nothing

Three honest choices exist, and each has trade-offs. None is right for everyone.

Salt-based softener

  • Swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium or potassium through ion exchange.
  • Actually removes the hardness minerals from the water it treats.
  • Needs salt refills, a drain connection and periodic regeneration.
  • Adds a small amount of sodium or potassium to treated water.
  • Best when scale on appliances and fixtures is a constant frustration.

Conditioner or doing nothing

  • A salt-free conditioner changes how minerals form scale but does not remove them.
  • Results vary by product and conditions, so ask for the maker's test data.
  • Doing nothing is a legitimate choice if you accept more cleaning.
  • Regular tank flushing and descaling can soften the toll on heaters.
  • A whole-house filter handles taste and chlorine but does not soften by itself.

Testing your own tap

A simple home check tells you whether to bother with a treatment, and it takes ten minutes.

1

Buy a hardness test kit

Pool and hardware stores sell test strips or drop kits that read in grains per gallon or parts per million.

2

Test a cold kitchen tap

Run the cold water for a minute, fill a clean cup and test it. Test before any softener if you already have one.

3

Compare with a published report

Ask your water utility for its water quality report and see how your reading compares with the range it lists.

4

Note the scale in your heater

Flushing a little water from the heater's drain valve into a bucket shows whether sediment and flakes are coming out.

Reading ppm and grains per gallon

One grain per gallon equals about 17.1 parts per million of hardness as calcium carbonate. So 145 ppm is roughly 8.5 grains per gallon, and 280 ppm is roughly 16.4. The USGS scale labels 121 to 180 ppm as hard, and anything above 180 as very hard, so a range from 145 to 280 spans both bands.

The range matters because it moves. Water at the low end behaves very differently from the top end. A softener sized for the average may still struggle in a stretch of higher readings.

A practical routine if you live with hard water

  • ✓Flush the tank water heater once a year, following the manufacturer's instructions for your model.
  • ✓Descale a tankless heater on the schedule in its manual, which often calls for a vinegar or approved cleaner circulation.
  • ✓Remove and soak faucet aerators and showerheads every few months in white vinegar.
  • ✓Run a dishwasher cleaner and use rinse aid if glasses cloud.
  • ✓Check the anode rod in a tank heater every few years, since it is the part that sacrifices itself to protect the tank.
  • ✓Keep a record of the date you flushed and descaled, so you can see how fast scale returns.

How a plumber helps with water treatment

Choosing equipment starts with the water you have and the plumbing it must serve. Water softener and filtration installation involves matching a unit to household size, choosing a spot with drain and power access and setting the regeneration schedule.

If scale has already damaged a heater, the next step might be replacing the water heater and adding treatment so the new one lasts longer. A plumber can also check pressure and inspect the heater before you spend money on a softener.

What makes water treatment cost more or less

Treatment prices vary because systems differ so much. The plumber explains the pieces and names the price before any work starts.

Type of system

A salt-based softener, a salt-free conditioner and a filter are different products with different parts and labor.

Household size and water use

A bigger household needs more capacity, which affects the size and price of the unit.

Installation location

A garage or utility closet with nearby drain and power is simple. A cramped or distant location takes more plumbing.

Bypass and plumbing changes

Adding bypass valves, outdoor hose bibb connections or a new drain line adds work.

Ongoing upkeep

Salt, filter cartridges and occasional service are part of the real cost over the years.

Hard water wear is gradual, so insurance does not usually cover it. Check your policy if you suspect sudden water damage from a failed appliance.

Coronado local note

Coronado note: what the 2023 water report says

California American Water's 2023 water quality report for its Coronado system lists the hardness of the treated water at 145 to 280 ppm as calcium carbonate, which is 8.5 to 16.4 grains per gallon. On the USGS scale that is hard to very hard. The practical result is that scale protection is worth planning for on any water heater and on fixtures you do not want to replace early.

The same report puts pH in the Coronado distribution system at 7.9 to 8.2, a slightly alkaline range. That is not a reason to treat the water, but it is a useful number to hand to a plumber when you discuss a softener or filter.

The report also says the supply is treated surface water purchased from the City of San Diego, from the Alvarado and Otay treatment plants. Since two plants feed the range, readings can vary, so test your own tap now and again rather than relying on a single number.
